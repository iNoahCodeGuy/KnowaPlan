Why the stamp and the state can't be one write

Lesson 3 · ~10 minutes · The reason settle_event commits exactly where it does

Back to Row 2 of Lesson 1 — the one you learned to fear. You pressed Settle, the page hung, the wifi dropped. Marcus's row read state=none with the charge stamp set: we'd written down “about to take $30” and never heard back. You learned to recognise it and press Retry.

This lesson is the other half: why that row is allowed to exist at all — and why it's the honest design, not a hole in it. By the end you'll look at the one await session.commit() inside settle_event and know that moving it three lines down would be a money bug.

The wish

Wouldn't it be nicer if there were no gap? Do the whole thing as one indivisible step: stamp the intent, charge the card, write paid — all together, so a crash leaves you either fully done or fully untouched, never half. No dangling row, ever.

You designed the thing this lesson explains. decisions.md 2026-07-13, “Record-first money movement.” You wrote why; here is the mechanism underneath it.

That indivisible step has a real name in your database. It's called a transaction, and understanding it is the whole lesson.

What a transaction actually is

A transaction groups a set of database writes so they land all together or not at all. The word for that property is atomic — indivisible. You commit() to make the whole group permanent; you rollback() to throw the whole group away.

You already lean on this. mark_attendance flips the RSVP machine (going → attended) and the attendance machine (unconfirmed → present) in one commit — so the database can never show one without the other. “A charge and its matching attendance update happen together” — that word together, in your own rules, means one transaction.

Here's atomic being atomic. Two writes — mark Marcus present, stamp the $30 intent — with a crash wedged between them. I ran this (sqlite3, nothing mocked):

start:                  (0, None)
crash (lost wifi at the court):
after crash + rollback: (0, None)   <- present rolled back to 0 too
after clean commit:     (1, 3000)
The thing to notice The first write — present = 1 — succeeded. It ran, it was real inside the transaction. Then the crash hit and rollback() undid it anyway, back to 0, because it was never committed. That's atomic: uncommitted work isn't “mostly done,” it's nothing.

Why the wish is impossible

Here's the catch that kills the one-write dream. A transaction can only roll back things that live inside your database. Your rollback() can un-write a row. It cannot un-charge a card.

Stripe is not inside your database. It's another company's computers, reached over the network — the same boundary Lesson 2 was about (your platform account is one side, Stripe the other). So the instant you put “charge the card” between two DB writes and keep the transaction open, you've built a trap.

Watch the trap spring. This reads in_transaction() — is the database still mid-transaction, uncommitted? — at the exact moment the (stand-in) Stripe call fires:

[    one-write] at the Stripe call, db.in_transaction() = True
[ record-first] at the Stripe call, db.in_transaction() = False

The one-write line is the trap. in_transaction() = True means the stamp is written but not committed while money is moving. If the process dies right there — after Stripe takes the $30, before your commit() — the database rolls the stamp back to nothing, and now money moved that your database has no record of.

This is the one outcome your rules forbid “The DB must always know at least as much as Stripe.” One-write breaks it in the worst direction: Stripe knows about $30, your database knows about nothing. You'd find out by angry text and reconcile it by hand in the Stripe dashboard — exactly the 8pm-at-the-court nightmare MISSION.md is written against.

So you pick which side of the gap to be safe on

You can't make the charge atomic with the writes — the charge is at Stripe, outside the transaction's reach. The gap is unavoidable. What you get to choose is which side of it you're standing on when a crash hits:

Charge first, record after Crash in the gap → money moved, no DB trace. Invisible. The forbidden outcome.
Record first, charge after Crash in the gap → stamp committed, state still none: a dangling row. Visible, queryable, retryable.

Record-first is the record-first line above: in_transaction() = False at the Stripe call, because you committed the intent first. If the process dies now, the stamp survives. The gap still exists — that surviving stamp with no answer yet is Row 2, the dangling charge — but now it's a known unknown: “we said we'd take $30; we don't yet know if it landed.” The database knew before Stripe did.

That's the honest option, and it's the only one. Not because the gap went away — because you moved it somewhere you can see.

Here it is in your real code (settle_event), trimmed to the bones:

payment = Payment(
    ...,
    charge_requested_at=datetime.now(timezone.utc),
    charge_requested_cents=charge,
)
session.add(payment)
await session.commit()     # <- intent durable HERE, before any money moves
...
await _collect(session, payment, attendee, planner, cents, ...)  # Stripe

That commit() above the Stripe call is the entire design. Slide it below _collect and you've rebuilt charge-first — the invisible-gap version. Which is why there's a test standing guard over its position, and we'll meet it in the quiz.
