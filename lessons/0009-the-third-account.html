The third account

Lesson 7 · ~10 minutes · You refunded two charges on 7/21 and told me it was done. It wasn't — the money is still sitting in an account you forgot you have.

A refund is the only correction you get on 7/29. Settlement runs once — no in-app re-marking after it fires (decisions.md). So when a charge is wrong, your only move is to refund it in the Stripe dashboard. This lesson is about the half of that move you can't see.

Start with what you actually did

Two real charges went through your live account on 7/21. You refunded both, in good faith, and reported them complete. Here is what your books say right now:

charge · saved card$1.00 · fee $0.33 · full $1.00 transferred out
refund$1.00 back to the card · fee $0.33 kept by Stripe
transfer tr_3TvdcV…reversed: false — the $1.00 never came back

transfer tr_3Tve3B…reversed: false — the $1.66 never came back
Stop — before you scroll Both cardholders got every cent back. The refunds succeeded. So what is wrong with this picture? You now spot this kind of hole on your own — take ten seconds, then tell me your answer. The rest of the lesson is the mechanism behind it.

The word doing all the work is reversed: false. To understand why that's a problem — and why it was invisible to you — you need the one thing the code lessons never taught, because it doesn't live in the repo. It lives in Stripe.

There are three accounts, not one

“My Stripe” feels like one balance. It is three parties, and a refund only touches two of them.

Every charge KnowaPlan makes moves money between three separate parties. Not two. The bug hides in the third.

AccountWhoRole in one charge
Cardholder Your friend Pays their share. A refund returns their money here — and this part is always visible and intuitive.
Platform You, as KnowaPlan The charge is created here, so it lands in your balance first. Stripe's fee is debited here. A refund is paid out of here. This is the account Lesson 2 showed going negative.
Connected The planner (also you, for now) Where the money is supposed to end up. transfer_data moves the full share here the instant the charge succeeds. A refund does not touch this account.

Read that last cell again. It is the whole lesson. When you refund, Stripe takes the money out of your platform balance to make the cardholder whole — but the dollars you already transferred are sitting in the connected account, and the refund leaves them exactly where they are.

Follow your $1.00 through all three

Watch the platform balance and the connected balance as your own $1.00 charge moves. Every number here is the 2.9% + 30¢ rate on a dollar.

StepPlatform (you)Connected (planner)
Charge $1.00 succeeds +$1.00 in, −$0.33 fee, −$1.00 transferred out = −$0.33 +$1.00
Refund $1.00 (what you did) −$1.00 to the card = −$1.33 still +$1.00
Reverse the transfer (what you skipped) +$1.00 pulled back = −$0.33 −$1.00 = $0.00

Three things to take from that table:

1. The cardholder is whole either way. They paid $1.00, they got $1.00 back. Nothing you skipped affects them — which is exactly why the dashboard let you feel finished.

2. The reversal is what empties the connected account. Without it, a full dollar you refunded is stranded in the planner's account. With it, that account goes back to zero, as if the charge never happened.

3. You are still out the 33¢ fee, forever. Look at the platform's final row: −$0.33, reversal or not. Stripe keeps its fee on a refund — verified, quoted at the bottom. A refunded charge always costs you the fee. That's not the bug; that's just the price of a refund.

Why it looked fine — and why that's the trap

Here's the sharp part. Solo, both the platform and the connected account are your pockets. Add up your final numbers: platform −$0.33, connected $0.00 with the reversal — or platform −$1.33, connected +$1.00 without it. Both combine to the same −$0.33. The reversal moved no net money for you.

This is precisely why you skipped it and felt done When you are both planner and platform, the reversal changes nothing you can see — same bottom line, same bank. The refund was visible and intuitive; the reversal was neither. So you did the visible half, reported complete, and you were right about everything you could observe.

Now put a second planner on the connected account. Refund your friend's $30 charge, skip the reversal, and the arithmetic stops being bookkeeping:

Your platform balance pays the refund−$30.00
The $30 you transferred sits in their account+$30.00
Net for you−$30.00, gone
Net for the other planner+$30.00 they shouldn't have

You ate the refund out of your own balance while the money you were correcting sits in someone else's account. The reversal is the only thing that pulls it back. Skip it and you have quietly paid a stranger $30 to refund your own friend.

Why this is the 7/29 lesson, not a someday lesson Settlement is one-shot — no in-app re-mark. If you charge the wrong person, or a walk-in lowers a share after a charge landed, the dashboard refund + reversal is the entire correction toolkit. A refund alone is a half-correction that reads as done. On club night you need the reflex: refund, then reverse, then read reversed: true.

Refund and cancel payments — Stripe

The paragraph on connected accounts states this lesson in one sentence: for a charge with transfer_data, “the destination account retains the funds by default, which leaves your platform account to cover the negative balance from the refund. To pull back the funds from the connected account, set the reverse_transfer parameter to true.” And on the fee: Stripe’s fees-for-refunded-payments page — “payment processing… fees from the original charge are not returned.”
